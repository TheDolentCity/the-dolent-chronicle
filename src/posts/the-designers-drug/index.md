First off, I'm a loser game designer who went to college for game design. That means I have very expensive opinions and I am more at fault for the behavior I'm about to describe than others, so don't yell at me.

## The Drug

Game designers crave cleverness. They can't get enough of it. Whether you're a level designer, systems designer, adventure designer, or whatever, there's a certain feeling you get when everything lines up in a perfect way. The design just _sings_. Tinkering with a design to try and get that feeling is like picking a lock in Skyrim—a little jittery at first, and you break a few tools trying to get something to bend, but when that oiled hinge whines and the design clicks "open" you feel immense satisfaction.

My metaphor might be stretched, but if you sit around in an RPG discord long enough, you'll observe this phenomena with dice systems. Folks will ecstatically discuss the ramifications of all player characters rolling 4d6 while all enemies roll 2d12. A newer designer will ask how they can improve player autonomy with dice rolls in their Odd-like system with a meta currency they've created. A well-respected designer from the dragon game space will discuss how their new dice system with two different colored dice that have less than twenty sides is very original and its cool because you use both the sum and the individual faces to determine the mixed success results of a check _(they tell the audience this has never been done before)_.

Lurkers often swarm out of the shadows and pounce in these discussions, throwing out college level statistics or unholy [AnyDice](https://www.anydice.com/) formulas. These discussions are game design crack for many, and they can be quite fun, but I do not think they produce great design with any sort of consistency.

Maybe you agree but you're unsure why I'm rambling about this? Its because I love minimalist RPGs and I think unnecessary system complexity is a consistent blight that cultivates this designer cleverness. When your system is made up of 6 different dice, 6 dice symbols, 2+ tokens, and 10 playing cards, you are able to combine, recombine, and twist mechanics in a thousand different shapes. Designing this kind of game provides the same sort of high as spending 8 hours building a License Level 6 Lancer mech. But I think in most cases the game would be improved by trimming this gristle.

## Seduction, then Reduction

One of the first long-running campaigns I played in utilized Fantasy Flight Games's _Age of Rebellion_ and _Edge of the Empire_. This system relied on 6 different dice, 6 dice symbols, 2 tokens, and long skill trees. Skill checks could commonly be 6-10 dice, with unique symbol results triggering different weapons, abilities, and talents. The result? A unique game that at first glance (or play) appears innovative and interesting. System mastery isn't needed, as discovering how to interpret these dice pools and utilize the symbols most effectively is part of the joy.

Fast forward 3 months. Another roll with 3 success and 2 threat. Your GM uses the 2 threat to inflict 2 strain on you. You are 6 away from hitting your threshold, but this fight is almost over so you can easily reset it. Your friend rolls 1 success and 3 advantage on an attack. They know they can use that to crit, that's almost certainly the most effective option, and they're bored, so they go with that.

Interpreting dice pools takes a lot of time and mental effort from the GM, but the game calls for these dice rolls often. Even if a fight with 4 PCs and 2 Stormtrooper squads lasts 2 rounds, it can still easily be 60 different dice rolled with odd symbols to interpret. Regular groups are going to resort to the fastest possible interpretations for most of these. You end up with a system that is interpreted very similarly to a 1d6 roll with 1-2 as failure, 3-4 as mixed success, and 5+ as strong success. The only difference is that you have to spend 10x longer adjudicating each roll.

Your _AoR_ and _EotE_ games might have run differently, but what I observed from my own game was most roll being resolved as quickly (and boringly) as possible, with the exception of key narrative situations. The table took loving time to interpret the 1 success, 2 advantage, and 1 despair result for the sniper shot at the high-ranking Imperial officer. The table was thrilled by negotiating the 1 failure, 3 threat, 2 triumph result on hacking a prison system's mainframe. In those moments, the design _sang_, but these moments were few and far between. In an isolated testing environment, systems like this one are quite **clever**, but it would be better if they weren't. We spent so much time resolving things that no one cared about and had nothing to do with lasers, starships, or droids. The cleverness demanded attention, it demanded energy—energy that should have gone into making more Star Wars.

## Design Small

Have you had this feeling of cleverness before? What about a feeling of dismay from seeking cleverness? Have you worked on a game for a while trying to perfect it, only to realize that you've simply added more mechanics and systems with a similar level of enjoyment as before? Have you spun your wheels and felt like nothing worked, trying to recreate a perfect session you ran previously to no avail?

I have, and I could be projecting, but I think many other designers have too. As humans we want to excel, to be original, to be creative, to be **triumphant!** Designers want to build something that hasn't been done before and deliver a game so clever it wows all their friends. The truth is, we don't need to. The first thing I see everyone removing from most games is the cleverness. Have you played _Dread_ rules-as-written? Probably not, but the Jenga Tower is awesome! Have you ignored half or more of a game's combat rules because you either didn't care to read them or they just seemed like too much? Have you run a campaign for a game you didn't even read 100% of the rulebook for? Have you flipped a coin and ignored the system when the moment called for it? It is an easy thing to ignore another designer's cleverness, why is it so hard to ignore your own?

Take this as a call to action: **design small, design well, and avoid the lure of cleverness.**
