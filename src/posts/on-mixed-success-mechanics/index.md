Mixed success mechanics are everywhere in tabletop RPG design. _Powered by the Apocalypse_ has its infamous 2d6 roll with 7-9 as a mixed success, its effect move-dependent. Fantasy Flight Games's _Age of Rebellion_ and _Genesys_ systems are famous for their **proprietary** mixed success dice; six colorful polyhedrals with combinations of six different symbols on each face. The _24XX_ series of games utilize a 1d6 roll with 3-4 as a setback or partial success.

The point is, if you've played more than 2 RPGs, you've likely played a game with a dice system that uses mixed success. But are they worthwhile?

## First Principles

Most of us have played with a shitty Game Master at some point. Someone who thought it was their job to punish players, to win, or to enforce a model of ludo-logical fairness that flies in the face of all rationality or social grace. Many of them play the dragon game, which uses a system of 1d20+mod to determine success.

Have you ever been in a situation where the GM told you to make a skill check doing something trivial, you roll a 1 (critical failure), and the GM took great joy in explaining how your level 8 wizard cut off both hands trying to make breakfast? Maybe it wasn't that extreme, but I expect _something_ like this has happened to most of us. This always feels bad and deeply silly. But this type of refereeing often extends in a more subtle way towards _all_ binary skill checks.

## The Binary Is Binary

Consider this situation:

```markdown
You are playing the dragon game. You are a level 5 goliath fighter with +4 Strength, a proficiency bonus of +3, training in the Athletics skill, and a background where you grew up in a mountain range. The Dragon Master describes a tall, craggy rock wall. At the top lies Castle Dunhall, ancestral home of House McGuffin and the destination for your quest. You and the rest of the party agree to climb the rock wall and the Dragon Master calls for a DC 10 athletics check for the whole party. With a +7 to the roll, you only fail on a 1 or 2. You roll a 2. FAILURE.

## What do you think should happen next?

1. The Dragon Master describes you climbing to the top and slipping at the last possible moment. It is at this point they mention the wall is 200 feet high and they roll 20d6 bludgeoning damage as you and the rest of the table argue that you weren't told it was that high before you climbed. The Dragon Master laughs and you get a fresh character sheet while you imagine a world with better friends.

2. The Dragon Master describes you falling halfway up the wall and rolls some damage dice behind the screen. You are left at 3 HP, and the Dragon Master asks if you want to try and climb again.

3. The Dragon Master describes your friends ascending while you struggle to get more than ten feet off the ground. The Dragon Master then says you are unable to climb this wall, but maybe you can find some other way around. The game stalls out for everyone else as you try and find a way past this obstacle by yourself.

4. The Dragon Master begins to sweat. They ask you to roll a dex save to catch yourself. You roll a 2. FAILURE. They ask you to roll a perception check to spot something to grab on as you fall. You roll a 1. FAILURE. Another player chimes in and asks if they can give the help action. The Dragon Master nods vigorously and you reroll with advantage. You roll a 3. Success. The Dragon Master wipes away the sweat and narrates how despite falling you eventually catch yourself and climb up.
```

There are infinite ways to adjudicate a failure, but of these options, I would guess you consider `2` or `3` the most reasonable. But is it fun? No. Why is it that you get everything you want, or nothing at all. Why does your expert, athletic mountain climber fall just as hard as the -2 strength Wizard? Maybe there is a way to have a result with _partial_ success that allows you to [fail forward](https://gnomestew.com/failing-forward-how-to-make-failure-interesting-in-rpgs/)?

## The Yellow Brick Road

I would guess a _Powered by the Apocalypse_ game is the first mixed success dice experience for many players, in part because there are so damn many of them. One of the strengths (and weaknesses) of these systems are their moves. Some are done well, some are done poorly, but pretty much all of them follow the format of:

- `1-6` Failure.
- `7-9` Partial success.
- `10+` Full success.

Consider this port of the dragon game above to a PbtA fantasy game:

```markdown
You are playing Powered by the Dragon. You are a goliath fighter with +3 Strength and a background where you grew up in a mountain range. The Dragon Master describes the scene above, and this time calls for a roll for the Avoid Danger move for the whole party. With a +3 to the roll, you only fail if the sum of your 2d6 roll is 3 or lower. You roll a 1 and a 3, for a total of 7. Partial Success.
```

PAUSE. Let's now pretend you're an amateur GM. All players got 10+ except for this one player. The text in the move tells you to "give a worse outcome or offer a hard choice." RESUME.

```markdown
## What do you think should happen next?

1. The Dragon Master describes you ascending the cliff face, but halfway up your trusty handaxes fall out of your pack to the ground below. You can choose to ascend or descend, but if you descend you won't have enough energy to scale the entire cliff face today.

2. The Dragon Master describes you ascending the cliff face, but it is taking you much longer than the others. You will summit, but while the rest of the party waits for you a random encounter roll will occur.

3. The Dragon Master describes you struggling to ascend the cliff face, and falling several feet onto a ledge you passed earlier. You take 1 harm, but are able to finally ascend the cliff.
```

All three of these options are improvements over the originals. Each is a "yes and..." or "yes, but..." whereas the 1d20 system was a binary "yes" or "no". The party can make forward progress,but with some costs, and the result is not immediately disastrous. Hallelujah! We've fixed tabletop RPGs forever. In all seriousness, I think mixed success systems and more prescriptive results are really useful for amateur GMs.

#### `QUESTION`

_How do I adjudicate the Paladin jumping in front of the Dragon's fire breath to protect the Wizard?_

#### `SYSTEM`

_Use the Protect Someone move to prevent harm to a character. On a 1-6, you fail. On a 7-9, the character suffers no harm and you suffer it all instead. On a 10+, the character suffers no harm and you suffer a little instead._

#### `ANSWER`

_Roll 2d6+Strength. On a 1-6, the Wizard takes 2 harm. On a 7-9, the Paladin takes 2 harm. On a 10+, the Paladin takes 1 harm._

If the above question was posed in a binary d20 system, an amateur might choose to have the Paladin negate all damage, or negate no damage, or take some amount of damage even on a successful roll. Maybe that would start an argument. Was the success "negating" damage, or "redirecting" the damage to the Paladin? If they rolled higher would they negate more damage? How does a GM decide what to do when their friend is staring at them and giving them no time to think? Ah, well they can fall back onto the system.

The system is the authority. The system tells you how to resolve

But what about a mixed success when you attempt to establish a new town? What about a mixed success while you investigate a clue in a mystery? What about a mixed success when you try to shoot a gun out of an opposing gunslinger's hand and have it fall on the ground and bounce six times to misfire and shoot all six of the bad guys?

## The Facade Cracks

Valeria notes in her [_Daggerheart_ review](https://www.valerialoves.com/daggerheart-a-dishonest-review-full-of-lies/#duality-dice-take-the-stress-out-of-mixed-successes) that "Creating compelling mixed success results roll after roll is often cited as the most stressful part of modern storygaming."

## The Designer's Drug

First off, I'm a loser game designer who went to college for game design. That means I have very expensive opinions and I am more at fault for the behavior I'm about to describe than others, so don't yell at me.

Game designers crave cleverness. They can't get enough of it. Whether you're a level designer, systems designer, adventure designer, or whatever, there's a certain feeling you get when everything lines up in a perfect way. The design just _sings_. Tinkering with a design to try and get that feeling is like picking a lock in Skyrim, a little jittery at first, and you break a few too many picks trying to get something to bend, but as you make some progress, the oiled hinge whines, until finally the design clicks open.

My metaphor might be stretched, but if you sit around in an RPG discord long enough, you'll see this phenomena with dice systems. People will ecstatically discuss the ramifications of all player characters rolling 4d6 while all enemies roll 2d12. A newer designer will ask how they can improve player autonomy with dice rolls in their Odd-like system with a meta currency they've created. A well-respected designer from the dragon game space will discuss how their new dice system with two different colored dice that have less than twenty sides is very original and its cool because you use both the sum and the individual faces to determine the results of a check _(they tell the audience this has never been done before)_.

Lurkers often swarm out of the shadows and pounce in these discussions, throwing out college level statistics or unholy [AnyDice](https://www.anydice.com/) formulas. These discussions are game design crack for many, and they can be quite fun, but I do not think they produce great design with any sort of consistency.

Maybe you agree but you're unsure why I'm bringing this up? I'm rambling about this because I think system complexity cultivates this cleverness. When your system is made up of 6 different dice, 6 dice symbols, 2+ tokens, and 10 playing cards, you are able to combine, recombine, and twist mechanics in a thousand different shapes. Designing this kind of game provides the same sort of high as spending 8 hours building a License Level 6 Lancer mech.

## The Binary Is Not Binary

Checks in tabletop roleplaying games across the board come down to two things:

1. Degrees of Success
1. Chance of Success

Some games only care about degree, and some only care about chance. _Powered by the Apocalypse_ games care about chance with a +3 mod more likely to succeed than a -1, but they tend to care more about degrees of success. What does a 7-9 on a ship navigation move actually mean?

Many people are taught that the Dragon game, and other d20 games like it, only care about chance of success and that degrees of success only come in with critical success/failure. But this is incorrect: **A GM and their table of players can always adjudicate the degrees of success, regardless of system.**
