I'm a huge fan of Chris McDowall's work; not only has he created some of the most famous lightweight fantasy roleplaying games, he's also filled them with referee advice that's often missing from more mainstream games. Despite his many excellent takes, there is one line of advice in _Mythic Bastionland_ that I cannot disagree with more:

> When faced with a question not answered by rules, notes, or procedures, you improvise. Anything too beneficial or too harmful creates the feeling of an arbitrary world.

This idea is also expressed in Sandra Snan's [Blorb Principles](https://idiomdrottning.org/blorb-principles):

> Try to make [your improvisation] something that won’t help or harm the player characters too much.

On the surface, this advice is trying to ensure some level of fairness. As Sandra notes, a Game Master could throw "20 angry beholders" or "20 free healing potions" at players, rendering a situation impossible or trivial for players to resolve. And while I would agree both of those options are likely not great improvisations in most circumstances, I would argue that both have their place. If you're spying on a beholder conclave and end up falling through a skylight into the council chambers, maybe there are 20 beholders in there. If players go snooping through storage rooms in a well-stocked, Imperial war hospital, surely they could find a surplus of 20 healing potions.

## My Stance

Ignore fairness, **focus your improvisation on being true to the world above all else**. This is how writers write, why should RPGs be any different? Worlds can be tenebrous, lambent, and fickle, including our own.

Picture yourself in New York City; paint in the skyscrapers, the dirty streets, and the grand cacophony. It is night. The waxing moon is lightly obscured by clouds. You're almost home. Then a light on the horizon nearly blinds you. A nuclear weapon detonates off-shore. The EMP knocks out the city's electricity, including street lights. The analog car honking reaches new heights in the sudden blackout. There's no phones. There's no internet. There's no news. Almost no one really understands what just happened. You're trying to reach home and bump into someone in the dark.

What are the odds they're "neutral" towards you? Maybe they're a desperate mother who can't find their car, and they left their kid inside. It could be a young man screaming for help because his eyes were burned. The stranger could be terrified, and have no qualms about hurting you. The "truth" of the fiction will depend on the existing setting details and the prejudices of the GM. Is this world a humanist one, where people take risks to try and survive together? Or is this world a near-future fascist capitalist hellscape where everyone looks out for themselves?

## An Example

I'm writing about this because I improvised a key bit during my Mythic Bastionland game last Monday. The players found a ruin with a massive dead tree. An NPC told them earlier that they had found a green bull (a myth and a blessing) at the base of said tree. One of the knights decided to take their shovel and dig there. Of course I had not written in my prep what, if anything, was buried beneath the tree. What do you think they should find?

1. Nothing but dead roots.
2. Something mundane or not "too beneficial or too harmful".
3. Something true to the world.

If we consider the [ICI Doctrine](https://www.bastionland.com/2018/09/the-ici-doctrine-information-choice.html), my player made a choice (dig at tree base) based off of information they've gathered (magical creature found here) and as a table we all expect some level of impact. Option 1 provides no impact. If anything, it actually discourages players from making choices like this one in the future. Option 2 provides little impact. Maybe he could have found a dagger, or a hat, or another shovel. There is some payoff, but it lacks emotional weight or any real logic (who put that mundane object there?).

This is why I always choose option 3. I described the knight finding an enormous seed. This made complete sense to me within the fiction because we had already established:

1. The realm suffered from famine and the land struggled to grow crops.
1. The Owl Knight who ruled in that region had a connection to trees.
1. The Owl Knight wanted to make the barren lands fertile once more.
1. The Owl Knight had a vision of a green bull under a dead tree and sent their acolytes to find it.

If you combine all of that with the themes of death and rebirth (and a little bit of secret knowledge I won't expose in case my players are reading) it just _makes sense_. Now what can this seed do? Obviously I didn't pre-plan that either, but I have one strong idea. The players might come up with something that makes better sense in the fiction though.

Is that unfair? Maybe, but I don't care, and neither should you.

## Small Addendum

While I disagreed with that one piece of advice in Mythic Bastionland, Chris goes on to write the following which is still great advice for improvisation:

> Describe something that does at least one of the following:<br/>
> • Evokes the flavour and themes of the area and its inhabitants.<br/>
> • Indulges their senses. Vision, hearing, touch, smell, and taste.<br/>
> • Reinforces something that they've already learned about this place.
