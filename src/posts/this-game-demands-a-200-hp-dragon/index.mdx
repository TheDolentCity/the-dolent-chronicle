My first tabletop roleplaying game was _D&D 4th edition_. I would wager most folks have a dragon game as their first, but mine was one of the crunchiest, most-hated editions. In many ways, 4e is closer to a [skirmish wargame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man-to-man_wargame) than a roleplaying game. Creating characters and optimizing builds is emphasized more than actual play in the community. The main 4e Discord server has a `build-chat` channel that's more popular than most other channels combined. The `looking-for-game` channel sits quietly, waiting for the infrequent poster who is brave enough to actually play the game.

Last April, I decided to run a new campaign using 4e. I had this idea for a ["Monster of the Week"](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MonsterOfTheWeek) dark fantasy game with both investigation and monster fighting elements. If tabletop roleplaying systems are engines, then I thought of 4e as a 16 cylinder combat engine; an appropriate fit for fighting monsters, plus everyone who signed up loves 4e or 5e and regularly begs me to run more games like them. In my mind, this all just made sense.

I was wrong.

Every game I run is [ruled by fiction, not systems](/manuscripts/play-worlds-manifesto#ruled-by-fiction-not-systems). A rogue may auto-kill a guard with a stealth check. A low-level wizard might auto-fail a ritual to banish an ancient curse. A fighter might get bonus damage because they used the right weapon for hunting a boar. As a GM, this makes stories and imaginary worlds enjoyable for me. This is not what everyone wants though, and what I've realized today is that it is definitely not what every *system* wants.

## Consider the Wyvern

I ran a session last night where the party helped defend an Imperial fort from an attack by a wyvern and a cult of drake knights. There was no mystery this time because I have been sick for over a week 💀, and didn't have as much time as I would like to prep. It was a straightforward brawl on the following battle map:

What I really want to focus on is the wyvern stats. I custom make most of the stat blocks in every game I run, and this game is no different. I read the wyvern stats in the Monster Manual and used those as a base, but I made several notable changes:

- Increased the fly speed from `8 (hover)` to `18 (fly)` with true flight where the wyvern can easily fly vertically in addition to horizontal movement.
- Added `Resist 10 fire, acid, and poison` damage.
- Added an acid blood property, where taking damage from a melee attack deals automatic acid damage in a close burst 1 around the wyvern.
- Added a weak flyer property, where the wyvern loses its fly speed when it is bloodied.
- Increased the damage of all attacks significantly. As a general rule 4e monsters lower than level 11 deal 1dX + Y damage on attacks. In this case, I increased every attack to PCdX + Y, the number of PCs determined the number of damage dice.
- Modified the bite attack into "Bite and Toss", grabbing a target on a hit and then making a secondary attack where they throw them, dealing more damage the further the target is thrown and inflicting some damage to whatever or whoever they crash into.
- Melee attacks into squads (a dozen or so NPCs represented by a single character token) deal full damage. This usually only applies to area attacks.

What is important here is ***why*** I made these changes. D&D has had a dragon problem for decades. Everywhere you look, you'll see people complain about how a fight with a dragon was underwhelming. A lot of this boils down to [problems with solo monsters](https://theangrygm.com/the-dd-boss-fight-part-1/), but even when a dragon does not fight solo, it often doesn't feel like a dragon. This is why posts like the [16 HP dragon](https://www.latorra.org/2012/05/15/a-16-hp-dragon/) and the [1 HP dragon](https://www.explorersdesign.com/the-1-hp-dragon/) are so popular. The people crave dragon fights! I wanted to deliver a dragon fight.

Wyverns to me are weaker, dumber dragons. Terrifying destructive threats, but not apocalyptic or cunning monsters to end a campaign with. They are not "two squares" faster than a PC, they are able to fly over an entire town in seconds. They don't bite or claw one soldier in a group, they eviscerate a dozen men in one fell swoop. They don't fly down next to a hero character with a big pointy weapon and let them wail on them, they breath rotting poison from above or fly past an enemy and try to toss them off the fort ramparts. This is wyvern *fiction*, which I tried to achieve by changing the statblock.

I think I was successful. This was a problem.

## Consider the Tactics

At one point during the session, a player literally said "Okay well I cannot do anything because of how this combat was designed". They were frustrated because the wyvern at that moment was 50 feet above the ground and they were a melee striker. While they had a backup ranged weapon, it was unlikely to do much at all and all of their powers required melee weapons.

I understand their frustration. Furthermore, **I think it's warranted**. For many players, a 4e character sheet is filled with ["buttons"](https://blog.farirpgs.com/posts/mechanics-like-buttons-on-a-game-controller) and you derive joy from pressing the right one at a tactical moment for maximum effect. This player could not push their character's buttons. Later in the combat, the wyvern was bloodied by ballista fire and this player was able to approach the crash-landed wyvern and do incredible levels of damage with their battleaxe. The monster died shortly thereafter. At the start of the combat, the wyvern's huge flying speed combined with its powerful flyby attacks created an accurate *fiction*, but limited player *tactics*.

This striker wasn't the only player who had issues with their character buttons. Another PC was melee only and slower than most. Due to the size of the map and where she chose to start the fight, she had to spend many actions to cross the map to where the drake knights were attacking. Spending whole turns just moving is not fun for anyone either. Was I wrong to make the map that big? I did use a pre-made map I found online, but it was one I liked. I think it represents the real size of the fort that was in my head. In other words, the fort map is accurate *fiction*, but the difficulty in moving across it is bad for the *tactics*.

Another PC sat on a ballista and used that to attack the wyvern and enemy soldiers the entire fight. The ballistas had huge range, attack bonuses, and damage, but were easily destroyed. This "button" was a stronger tactical choice than anything on that PC's character sheet for fighting the flying wyvern, so why would they do anything else? Of course if you consider the *fiction*, a projectile siege weapon is always going to be stronger than a little bolt of fire against a dragon, but is this interesting at a *tactics* level?

I'm frustrated because on some level I knew all this already. ***I*** **gave this wyvern 200 HP**. I knew my players could still kill it in a few rounds and I was right. If I had given it a reasonable amount of HP I could've leaned *even more* into the fiction. The wyvern might've ended each of its turns within the fog, unable to be seen. It might've picked off the ballistas one-by-one, so it couldn't have been shot. It might have hid in the fog until its powerful breath weapon recharged. I didn't do those things though. I gave it a large health pool because ***that's the correct way to play this game***. If the dragon didn't have that much HP, then players would not have time to press the buttons on their character sheet. It needs enough bulk so that the Avenger can use Menacing Strike, the Ranger can use Attacks on the Run, and the Runepriest can use Rune of Endless Fire. Otherwise, why do the players have these buttons at all?

## Questions without Answers

Who do I serve as the GM? The fiction? The ruleset? The tactics? My players?

What should be my goal? To portray the fiction reliably? To follow the rules-as-written for maximum fairness? To present complex encounters that encourage tactical thinking? To make sure my players have a fun time?

What if one goal betrays another? If a wyvern flying above a battlefield is a problem, do you find some excuse for it to land? If an enemy is too far away, do they move closer so a PC can hit them? Is this coddling? Is this engaging? Is it fair? Does it even matter if it makes people happy?

## Truth without Comfort

I always thought of games like 4e and Lancer as crunchy systems for combat. That's not true. They are not mechanical systems that use some computation to resolve ambiguous situations; they are systems that demand specific situations that can be resolved with specific abilities players chose. They demand tactical positions that exist irrespective or in flagrant violation of the fiction. They demand I design a video game.

I guess it's a good thing I have a degree in that at least.
