For me, the world of _Halo_ came to life on the playground. I had never played the game series—I didn't even know they were games. Halo was a conjuration of a friend, a new space for imaginative free play in the wide, wet field of recess. I learned about the parasitic Flood with their misshapen bodies and desire to consume the whole galaxy. I discovered the Library, a vast Forerunner megalith with the life-saving Index at its core. I was introduced to the Warthog, a futuristic military jeep with a mounted chaingun, big enough and fast enough for me and my friends to outrun the Flood.

I didn't play my first Halo video game until years later. It was love at first sight. Now I had images and sounds that I could tie into my previous conceptions of elites, hunters, and banshees. In high school, I blitzed my way through every Halo novel (far more than you think at the time). I won't lie, some of them were very bad, but I found stories within those pages that could stand up to even the greatest sci-fi epics.

The more cynical may say that Halo is a corporate IP that Microsoft wants to squeeze as much money out of as possible; a series with a faceless male protagonist and conventional military sci-fi setting. In some ways, they'd be correct. But the Halo universe is vast and filled with brilliant writers. In _Stomping on the Heels of a Fuss_, you follow an imprisoned intelligence officer as he studies the social dynamics of his alien captors in the hopes he can use their behavior to escape. In _Halo: Glasslands_, a secret team of space marines are tasked with destabilizing an alien ally of humanity by arming revolutionary insurgents (a narrative that has no resemblance to our actual real life of course). The games themselves are filled with exceptional writing, featuring moments like a hive mind of Flood forms saying "I am a monument to all your sins" or Master Chief getting permission to "give the Covenant back their bomb" before pushing a bomb towards an enemy cruiser in the middle of a naval battle. There are moments of slapstick marine comedy, moments of heroic resilience as you jump a warthog into a moving ship to avoid an explosion, but also moments of pure heart.

At the end of _Halo: Reach_, you have one last mission. Your squad mates are dead, and Reach has fallen. But not for nothing, you succeeded, setting up victory for humanity in the games that follow. The game does not give you much detail about what's going on. The heroic, orchestral music is gone. No one is there to talk to you over comms. There are no orders. A small blip of text appears on the screen: "OBJECTIVE: Survive". You hold out as long as you can. Your helmet cracks and the game UI disappears from the damage. Eventually, you are overwhelmed and forced to watch as an alien drives an energy sword straight towards your face. The game hard cuts to your player's chosen helmet half-buried in the ground, a hole deep in the visor, and the surface of Reach turned to glass from orbital laser bombardment. A few moments pass, the image fades to black, and then returns, but time has passed and grass has begun to grow again. That's why I love Halo.
